# Economy

[I] ECONOMY-PRODUCTION1: employed residents produce local output, and healthier workers make employed labor more productive.

[I] ECONOMY-FARM1: agricultural workers produce food; fertile land and seasonal weather raise harvests, while pollution and water stress reduce them.

[I] ECONOMY-MANUFACTURING1: manufacturing workers turn local mineral potential into materials, while the Clean Air Act reduces material throughput.

[I] ECONOMY-SECTOR-RETURNS1: agricultural output per worker rises with food prices, service output rises with business health, sports output rises with sports interest, and clean-air rules slightly reduce manufacturing output.

[I] ECONOMY-CASHFLOW1: productive local activity brings private cash into the mapxel in proportion to its output.

[I] ECONOMY-TRADE1: neighboring populated land mapxels exchange food and materials from the place with more stock per resident toward the place with less.

[I] ECONOMY-TRADE2: better infrastructure between neighboring mapxels lets their food and material stocks equalize faster.

[I] ECONOMY-TRADE3: food traded between neighbors is valued at their average food price, while materials use a fixed trade price.

[I] ECONOMY-CONSUMPTION1: residents need one unit of food each month, and local food security is the share of that need that available food can satisfy.

[I] ECONOMY-SPOILAGE1: households consume some materials and part of any food left after eating spoils each month.

[I] ECONOMY-SPENDING1: household activity sends private cash out of the local economy according to population, local cash per resident, and output.

[I] ECONOMY-PRICE1: food scarcity and food insecurity push local food prices upward, while plentiful supply lets prices ease gradually.

[I] ECONOMY-PRICECONTROL1: food price controls prevent the market rule from raising local food prices above one.

[I] ECONOMY-BUSINESS1: food insecurity, expensive food, crime, and unaffordable service-sector payrolls weaken local businesses.
