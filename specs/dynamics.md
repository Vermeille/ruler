# Emergent pressure and crises

[I] UNREST-MOBILIZATION1: sustained hardship, low approval, pessimism, relative disadvantage, civic-liberty conflict, and mobilized neighboring communities can build latent political mobilization; visible unrest is lower when public assembly is restricted even though latent mobilization can continue growing.

[I] SERVICE-OVERLOAD1: health and education capacity adapt gradually to resident demand and funded public provision, so rapid population growth or unusually heavy demand can temporarily reduce effective access before capacity catches up.

[I] EPIDEMIC-SPREAD1: infection spreads through contact with local and neighboring populations, more readily under density and health-system strain, recovers faster with good health access, harms infected people's health, and travels with population groups when they move.

[I] CRISIS-DISPLACEMENT1: severe food insecurity, water stress, crime, poor health, or service disruption can create migration outside the ordinary quarterly cadence at a higher flow rate, and extreme hardship partly overcomes the usual inability of households with few reserves to move.

[I] REGIONAL-DIVERGENCE1: people who live in a region falling behind national wellbeing, or who fall behind comparable people around them, experience modest additional pessimism and political grievance that can feed migration and mobilization.

[I] POLICY-SHOCK1: abrupt changes in taxes, public spending, minimum wages, laws, or subsidies create temporary local adjustment pressure; people with more adaptable and risk-tolerant archetypes react less strongly, while material consequences still come from the underlying policy mechanics.

[I] EXPECTATIONS1: people's outlook remembers whether wellbeing, health, income, and wealth are improving or deteriorating; outlook affects approval, migration pressure, political mobilization, and reproductive decisions rather than people reacting only to current levels.

[I] PUBLIC-SALIENCE1: food shortages, health-system pressure and infection, crime, and education failures temporarily increase how strongly people attend to the corresponding existing need when judging wellbeing and migration destinations, then fade as conditions normalize.

[I] CASCADE-FAILURE1: sustained overload can create temporary health, education, or infrastructure disruption whose risk rises nonlinearly with utilization and weak underlying conditions; disruption degrades effective service and recovers faster when public provision remains funded.

[I] DISTRIBUTIONAL-REACTION1: groups react politically when their lived trajectory falls behind their region or local peers, so unequal outcomes can create grievances without hard-coded assumptions that a demographic group supports or opposes a particular policy.
